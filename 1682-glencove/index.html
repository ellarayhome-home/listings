What this is

This is not a cash purchase. The existing mortgage stays in place at its current rate and payment, and you step into it. You control the property from closing — you rehab it, you list it, and when it sells the existing loan is paid off out of your proceeds like any other payoff. It's gone at that point and the spread is yours.

You are not applying for financing and you are not qualifying for anything. You are stepping into a loan that already exists, at a rate that no longer does.

Mechanically it's held in a trust rather than deeded straight across, and you take the beneficial interest. That's the part most people haven't seen before, so the short version: it changes nothing about what you can do with the house, and it's the structure that keeps the existing lender where it is. I'll walk you through it on a call.

How it runs

  1. You review the file and run whatever due diligence you want on the property.
  2. We paper the assignment. Closing is handled through the trustee — there's no title company in the middle and no lender approval to wait on, so this moves fast.
  3. At closing you fund the reinstatement, the seller's cash, the assignment fee and the flat $7,300 closing fee. The beneficial interest transfers to you and you have control of the property. No transfer taxes, no third-party closing charges, no tax proration — the $7,300 is all of it.
  4. You make the monthly payment, on time, every month, for as long as you hold it.
  5. You rehab and list. At resale the existing loan is paid off from your proceeds.

Because there's no lender approval and no escrow period, this can close in days rather than weeks.

Possible exits

This house doesn't only work one way. Five that fit it, and what each looks like from where you'd be sitting.

  1. Fix and flip

    The straight play. Rehab it, list it, sell it. The existing loan is paid off out of your proceeds at closing and the spread is yours.

    Comps say roughly $275,000 as-is and $378,000 renovated; I underwrite at $335,000. Median days on market across the set is 124 — so budget six months of carry at $1,743.30, not three.

  2. BRRRR — with one warning

    Rehab, rent, refinance, repeat. It works here, but read this before you plan on it: a refinance pays off the 2.875% loan. You'd be trading a rate that doesn't exist anymore for whatever investor money costs today.

    If the plan is to hold, the stronger version is to skip the refi entirely — rehab it, rent it, and leave the cheap debt in place. Your all-in payment is $1,743.30 including taxes and insurance. The rent side is yours to run.

  3. Wrap to a homestead buyer

    Sell it on seller financing to an owner-occupant who can't qualify conventionally. There is no shortage of them in Brevard.

    You take a down payment, write the note above what you're paying, and keep the monthly spread while the 2.875% sits underneath. You exit at or above retail with no listing, no commission, and no 124 days on market.

  4. Co-living

    Rent by the room instead of by the house. 1,964 square feet across two floors, two bathrooms, and a fully fenced quarter acre — the layout supports it, and the Melbourne and Space Coast employers are a short drive.

    Room-by-room generally beats whole-house rent on the same asset, against the same $1,743.30 payment.

    Confirm Palm Bay's occupancy rules and the site's capacity before you count rooms.

  5. Sober living / recovery residence

    Same physical logic as co-living, different operator and a different demand curve. Quiet residential street, large fenced yard, two floors, separable spaces.

    Florida runs a voluntary certification track through FARR, and recovery residences carry protections under the Fair Housing Act that ordinary group-housing uses don't get. If you already operate one, you know the economics beat a standard rental.

    Same caveat as above — verify occupancy limits and site capacity first.
